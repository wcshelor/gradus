# Learning and teaching piano pieces

This reading list centers the thesis on a practical question:

> What makes this passage difficult for this learner, and what kind of support
> is most likely to help them learn it?

It is an exploratory evidence map, not a completed systematic review. Source
metadata and searchable tags are in `../bibliography/library.xlsx`.

## Working model: difficulty is a diagnosis, not a score

A learner can fail at the same notes for different reasons. The useful unit for
AI assistance is therefore a **learner–passage problem**, not merely a passage
with a high global difficulty value.

| Difficulty layer | Typical evidence | Pedagogical response to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Score decoding | hesitation, losing place, note/rhythm errors, excessive looking between score and hands | reduce visual load; identify patterns; annotate rhythm, harmony, voices, or landmarks; targeted reading work |
| Motor planning and fingering | unstable fingering, awkward transitions, inability to repeat reliably | settle and explain fingering; isolate transitions; test ergonomic alternatives; rehearse movement without tempo pressure |
| Coordination and timing | hands work separately but fail together; unevenness; polyrhythmic or voicing breakdown | isolate coordination relation; reduce voices or events; use coordination checkpoints; vary tempo or rhythm with a stated purpose |
| Auditory goal and monitoring | correct notes but weak balance, articulation, phrasing, or unnoticed errors | establish an aural model; compare attempts; direct attention to one audible feature; use feedback that supports self-evaluation |
| Musical structure and memory | starts only from the beginning; breakdowns at boundaries; fragile memorization | analyze form, harmony, and cues; practise starts and joins; retrieval practice; combine mental and physical rehearsal |
| Practice regulation | repeated run-throughs, unclear goals, failure to change strategy, stopping after every slip | set a local goal; choose a strategy; attempt; evaluate; adapt; schedule later retrieval and reintegration |
| Affective and task fit | avoidance, overload, low confidence, tension, inappropriate repertoire challenge | resize the task; preserve agency; select attainable subgoals; involve the teacher; avoid medical or biomechanical diagnosis by software |

These layers interact. A fingering problem can create timing errors; weak score
understanding can make memory fragile; inappropriate tempo can hide whether the
learner actually understands the coordination. A system should express this as
hypotheses and gather more evidence instead of announcing a single cause.

## Start here: direct evidence

1. **Duke, Simmons, and Cash (2009), “It’s Not How Much; It’s How.”** A compact
   piano study showing that retention was associated with how errors and target
   passages were handled, rather than practice time or number of repetitions
   alone. This is the clearest starting point for a diagnostic practice loop.
2. **Loimusalo and Huovinen (2021), expert pianists learning a new piece.** Read
   for the phases, priorities, and problem-solving perspectives pianists bring
   to unfamiliar repertoire.
3. **Suzuki and Mitchell (2022), play and non-play strategies of tertiary piano
   students.** Useful taxonomy for widening assistance beyond keyboard drills
   to score study, planning, reflection, and mental work.
4. **Lehmann and Ericsson (1998)** and **Williamon and Valentine (2000).** Read
   together for the changing organization and quality of practice during
   performance preparation.
5. **Chaffin and Imreh (2002)** and **Lisboa, Chaffin, and Demos (2015).** Core
   sources on structural understanding, deliberate performance cues, and the
   thoughts that organize memorization.
6. **Allingham and Wöllner (2022).** Slow practice is not one intervention; it
   serves different cognitive functions. Any tempo recommendation should state
   what slowing down is meant to reveal or stabilize.
7. **Parncutt et al. (1997).** An interpretable ergonomic model of melodic
   fingering, useful as a bridge from score features to explainable options.
8. **Lappe, Lappe, and Keller (2018).** Helps separate pitch feedback, sequence
   learning, and timing rather than treating “accuracy” as one construct.

## How learners learn to practise

Read **Hallam (2001)**, **Hallam et al. (2012)**, **Nielsen (2001)**, and
**McPherson and Renwick (2001)** as a group. Their central design implication is
that novices often do not yet possess the planning, monitoring, error diagnosis,
and strategy-selection skills assumed by expert advice. Assistance should model
those processes and gradually transfer control to the learner.

A useful interaction pattern is:

1. choose one musical or technical intention;
2. obtain a short baseline attempt;
3. identify the observable mismatch;
4. propose a likely cause and say what evidence supports it;
5. select one bounded strategy matched to that cause;
6. retry and compare on the intended criterion;
7. decide whether to retain, alter, or abandon the strategy;
8. reconnect the passage and schedule later retrieval.

This is more defensible than generating a fixed daily schedule from score
difficulty alone.

## Teaching, explanation, and feedback

Instrumental lesson research suggests that pedagogical usefulness depends not
only on whether advice is technically correct. Timing, wording, demonstration,
student cooperation, prior understanding, and opportunities for the student to
evaluate their own playing matter too.

Prioritize the review **Feedback in Music Performance Teaching (2022)**,
research on teacher directives in children’s instrumental lessons (2018), and
Burwell’s work on independence in studio teaching. Use **Error Management for
Musicians (2014)** to distinguish:

- preventing predictable errors;
- detecting and repairing errors during practice;
- recovering without collapse in performance;
- maintaining an error-friendly environment in which experimentation remains
  possible.

For an AI assistant, feedback should usually be specific, limited to a salient
goal, connected to observable evidence, and phrased to invite listening and
self-correction. A teacher-facing mode may expose alternatives and uncertainty;
a student-facing mode should not impersonate a definitive teacher.

## Teacher diagnosis in piano lessons

This search lane asks how teachers notice, interpret, and respond to learner
problems during lessons. The phrase "teacher diagnosis" is not always used in
the piano literature; relevant work is often indexed under feedback, correction,
studio interaction, teacher expertise, assessment, or sight-reading placement.

### Core piano-specific sources

1. **Blackwell, McPherson, Matherne, and Seavello (2026), “Teacher Feedback in
   Beginning and Intermediate Piano Lessons.”** Prioritize as the most direct
   current source on piano teachers' feedback to developing learners. Read for
   the balance among feed-back, feed-forward, and feed-up, and for the finding
   that teacher comments are dominated by task-level responses rather than
   process or self-regulation guidance. Thesis use: evidence that an assistant
   should help turn observed problems into actionable next steps and learner
   monitoring prompts.
2. **Siebenaler (1997), “Analysis of Teacher-Student Interactions in the Piano
   Lessons of Adults and Children.”** Add as a classic observational source on
   piano studio teaching. Read for teacher modeling, feedback, lesson pacing,
   brief teacher-student episodes, and expert ratings of lesson effectiveness.
   Thesis use: observable patterns in how teachers interrupt, model, correct,
   and shape short performance trials.
3. **Blackwell (2020), “Expertise in Applied Studio Teaching: Teachers Working
   with Multiple Levels of Learners.”** Read for how recognized applied teachers
   adjust side coaching, modeling, feedback, and correction across learner
   levels. The appended Duke and Simmons summary is especially useful for the
   idea that expert teachers select technically or musically important proximal
   goals that are attainable and audibly changeable in the moment.
4. **Duke and Henninger (2002), “Teachers' Verbal Corrections and Observers'
   Perceptions of Teaching and Learning.”** Use as a correction-wording and
   observer-perception source. It helps separate error identification, verbal
   framing, the instruction to retry, and actual diagnostic usefulness.

### Diagnostic and prescriptive practitioner sources

5. **Hardy (1998), “Teaching Sight-Reading at the Piano: The Diagnostic
   Prescriptive Sight Reading Program.”** Treat as practitioner evidence rather
   than controlled empirical proof. It is directly useful for diagnostic
   sight-reading: error charts, placement levels, remediation by error type,
   and a distinction between more and less serious reading errors.
6. **Iliadis (2021), “College Group Piano: A Teacher Training Manual with
   Supplemental Teaching Materials.”** Treat as a teacher-training and
   troubleshooting source for group piano. Read for common weak spots in
   beginning college group piano, diagnostic categories, and supplemental
   exercises that make teacher response more systematic.

### Adjacent instrumental sources to use carefully

Use instrumental-studio sources when they clarify diagnostic behavior that is
likely to transfer to piano, but label the transfer argument explicitly.
McPherson and Blackwell's collegiate instrumental feedback work, Matherne and
Blackwell's feedback guidance, and error-management work can help define
actionable feedback, student-to-teacher feedback, overload, neutral wording, and
error-friendly learning conditions.

### Open questions for the next search

- How do expert piano teachers infer causes behind the same surface error: score
  reading, fingering, coordination, listening, memory, confidence, or task fit?
- Which lesson observations record teacher questioning and probing, not only
  teacher correction?
- Do teachers diagnose differently for children, adult amateurs, undergraduate
  pianists, and advanced performers?
- Which teacher responses actually improve delayed retention or transfer, not
  just the next performance trial?
- Can diagnostic categories from sight-reading and group-piano placement be
  adapted to passage-learning support without reducing learning to error counts?

## Reading, movement, and coordination

Sight-reading studies by **Waters et al. (1998)**, **Furneaux and Land (1999)**,
and **Kim et al. (2019)** motivate a distinct score-decoding layer involving
pattern recognition, gaze, anticipation, and visual-motor strategy. Sight
reading is especially relevant to the initial encounter with a piece, but it
should not be used as a complete model of later repertoire learning.

Biomechanical work by **Furuya and colleagues** shows that expert piano movement
depends on coordinated joints, timing, and force control. This supports richer
descriptions than “finger weakness.” It does **not** by itself justify automated
posture correction, injury diagnosis, or prescriptive movement advice. Those
functions require specialist validation and explicit safety boundaries.

## Memory, spacing, and schedules

Memory research supports treating a piece as a network of structural,
interpretive, and motor retrieval cues rather than a chain that can only be
started at bar one. Practice plans should include starts, joins, mental recall,
and delayed retrieval, not only immediate repetition. The keyboard-melody study
by **Duke et al. (2009)** also makes rest placement and overnight consolidation
relevant to scheduling, while its laboratory scope cautions against pretending
to know an exact optimal schedule for a complete work.

## Supporting background, not the teaching core

The neuroscience literature—such as **Pascual-Leone et al. (1995)**,
**Herholz and Zatorre (2012)**, and **Hyde et al. (2009)**—supports the broad
claim that musical and keyboard training engage plastic perceptual-motor
systems. It rarely tells a teacher which intervention to use on a particular
bar. Keep it as explanatory background and as motivation for longitudinal,
multimodal learning, not as a source of “brain-based” prescriptions.

Likewise, far-transfer claims should be handled cautiously. **Schellenberg
(2004)** is historically important, while **Sala and Gobet (2020)** provide a
strong counterweight to claims that music lessons reliably create broad
cognitive or academic gains. The thesis can make a stronger contribution by
measuring piano learning directly.

## Pedagogical traditions and expert sources

Classic piano-pedagogy books and methods are worth a separate search because
they contain rich practitioner knowledge about sound, fingering, movement,
interpretation, and passage practice. They should be tagged as expert or
historical sources, not silently treated as experimental proof. Candidate
traditions include Neuhaus, Gieseking and Leimer, Sándor, Fink, Whiteside, and
later technique schools. The review should compare their prescriptions,
assumptions about learners, and compatibility with empirical motor-learning and
safety research.

## Thesis opportunities exposed by the search

- **Diagnostic representation:** represent a passage using multiple difficulty
  hypotheses and learner evidence rather than one predicted level.
- **Pedagogical matching:** map each hypothesis to candidate interventions,
  contraindications, required evidence, and a method of checking improvement.
- **Explainable assistance:** explain the musical or motor relation that makes a
  passage demanding, while marking uncertainty and learner dependence.
- **Generated drills:** transform only the relevant dimension, preserve the
  passage’s musical relationship, and provide a path back into the score.
- **Practice planning:** generate short goal–strategy–evaluation cycles and
  spaced revisits, not merely time allocations.
- **Teacher collaboration:** allow teacher correction, approval, and annotation;
  record why an intervention was selected.

## Highest-priority gaps for the next search

1. Controlled comparisons of common piano interventions: hands separate versus
   together, rhythmic variants, chaining direction, accents, blocking, and
   deliberate tempo progression.
2. Observational studies of how excellent piano teachers diagnose the same
   student error differently, especially sources that capture teacher probing
   and decision-making rather than only correction frequency.
3. Studies linking passage features and learner characteristics to intervention
   success, rather than only to difficulty ratings.
4. Evidence on learning expression and interpretation throughout acquisition,
   rather than adding musicality after note accuracy.
5. Longitudinal evaluations of automated feedback, generated exercises, and
   practice planners using retention and transfer—not just immediate accuracy.
